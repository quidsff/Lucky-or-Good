Points Over Record

A fantasy football essay

By @Quids_FF · Week 7, 2026

I don't think I'm alone. Making friends as an adult man can be tricky with all the responsibilities that pile up. I try to be a good father, a good husband, a good employee or business owner, like many of you, I assume. That doesn't leave a lot of time for making new friends. Fantasy football has been both an escape and a social oasis.

I'm grateful to have found a league that has lasted. I'm going on seven years as a member. Some folks have come and gone, but the core of us has stuck through. And we're from all over: several islands in the Bahamas, New England, the industrial belt, the South, and occasionally Canada. In some ways our group represents the best of what the internet has to offer, bridging distances and building relationships.

Despite the distance, we've managed to create a space where we can still playfully jockey for dominance within our competitive little group. And we jockey hardest when it comes to league tiers. One member, let's call him the accountant, takes stock of every team in the league and sorts us into tiers based on his own read of things: roster construction, star power, records, injuries, players punching above their weight. It's a real service, and it fuels the fun part: the debate once the tiers drop.

I'm no exception. But my complaint is always the same. The tiers should rest on criteria that are concrete, reliable, and knowable. And the single best input is points.

Points elegantly measure your team. Records are muddier. A record folds in how your team performed, how your opponent performed, and plain schedule luck. Take two teams, Team A and Team B, in a head-to-head matchup. Team A loses to Team B. But Team B posted the highest score in the league that week, and Team A posted the second highest. The record says Team A lost and Team B won. It doesn't say Team A was the second-highest scorer that week, better than ten other teams. So how do we make sense of this? Team A's loss says more about the schedule than the team. They weren't bad. They were the second-best team that week, and they still lost, simply because they played the wrong opponent. Over a season, these flukes, the unlucky losses and the lucky wins, pile up in your record. But they wash out when you look at points.

I can see my wife's face now, and I can hear her saying, so what? Why does this matter? Because every manager in every league, every season, has to answer the same question: do I stay the course or make a major change? And the time to answer it is now. Week seven is roughly the halfway point in most leagues, late enough that the numbers mean something, early enough that a change can still pay off. But you can't answer that question until you answer a harder one honestly: how good is my team, really? Points are the simplest and most honest answer we have.

The reason to be clear about this is that doing the best thing is often uncomfortable. If you're winning, it's hard to admit you've been lucky. If you're losing, it's hard to sit still and trust the numbers when every instinct says do something. Rules help, because they're decided before you feel the feelings.

So, here are some rules. First, find your league's playoff line: if six teams get in, the line is the sixth-best scorer by points per game. Are you above it or below it? If you're scoring like a playoff team and winning, congratulations, you're good, and the standings know it. Don't get cute. If you're scoring below the line and losing, there's no bad luck to blame. The record is telling the truth. Touch some grass, then make your changes with a clear head. The trickier cases are when points and record disagree. If you're scoring like a playoff team and expect to keep doing so, but you're losing, hold steady. The wins are coming. If you're winning but scoring below the line, maybe go buy a lottery ticket, because you've been lucky. But don't expect it to last. Make a change now, while the record still gives you leverage.

One more thing the rules assume: that you're still mathematically in it. If your record means you can't get in no matter how you score, holding steady is denial. Start thinking about next year. In a dynasty league like ours, next year is never that far away.

If you don't feel like doing the math, I built a tool that does it for you. Type in your Sleeper username and it plots your whole league on the grid, shows you the playoff line, and tells you which rule applies to your team: Lucky or Good?

None of this means record is useless. Record decides seeding, and seeding decides who plays in December. And the accountant's eye still matters: he sees the injuries, the bye weeks, the star coming back next week, things a points column can't. Points aren't the whole picture. They're the foundation you build the picture on. Start there, and the arguments get sharper, the trades get smarter, and the tiers get closer to the truth. Which, let's be honest, is all any of us wants: to be told, with evidence, that we're better than our record says. Or exactly as good as it says. Whichever one happens to flatter us this week.
